# The maths behind a GymRank rank

Every number GymRank shows comes from a published formula applied to one set of
yours. This document gives each formula, where it comes from, and — the part
that matters most — what it cannot tell you.

The engine lives in [`server/lib/scoring.js`](server/lib/scoring.js). It is a
pure module: no database, no clock, explicit inputs and outputs, so all of it
can be checked on its own. The constants are in
[`server/lib/scoring-config.js`](server/lib/scoring-config.js) and the derived
thresholds in [`server/lib/thresholds.js`](server/lib/thresholds.js), which is
generated by [`server/scripts/calibrate.js`](server/scripts/calibrate.js).

A set becomes a rank in six steps:

```
bar load + bodyweight factor
        ↓  effective load
estimated 1RM           Epley and Brzycki, averaged
        ↓
DOTS points             ÷ P(bodyweight)
        ↓
age-adjusted score      × Foster or McCulloch coefficient
        ↓
strength index 0–1000   interpolated between calibrated anchors
        ↓
rank and division       Iron V … Unkillable Demon King I
```

---

## Step 1 — Effective load

```
external lifts     load = weight on the bar
bodyweight lifts   load = bw_factor × bodyweight + added weight
```

`bw_factor` is the fraction of bodyweight the movement actually moves:

| Exercise | `bw_factor` | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Pull-up, dip | 1.00 | essentially the whole body is lifted |
| Push-up | 0.70 | force-plate studies report about 69% of bodyweight in the up position and 75% in the down position; 0.70 is the usual midpoint |
| Squat, bench, deadlift, overhead press | 0 | the bar is the load |

Added weight may be negative, for band assistance. If the effective load comes
out at zero or less the set is stored but not scored.

**Limitation.** A single factor per exercise ignores limb lengths, and the
push-up figure in particular is a population midpoint, not a measurement of
you. A back squat also moves part of your own mass, which this model ignores
because the published strength standards it is calibrated against also ignore
it — the two conventions have to agree or the anchors mean nothing.

---

## Step 2 — Estimated one-rep max

One rep is already a maximum, so it is used unchanged. Between 2 and 12 reps
the estimate is the mean of two formulas:

```
Epley     1RM = load × (1 + reps / 30)                 Epley, 1985
Brzycki   1RM = load × 36 / (37 − reps)                Brzycki, 1993
```

They are averaged because they err in opposite directions: Epley reads high at
low rep counts and Brzycki reads low at high ones. At exactly ten reps they
coincide, which is a useful thing to check an implementation against — both
give 133.33 kg for 100 kg × 10, and a test pins that.

**Above 12 reps the set does not feed the rank at all.** Past that point the
two formulas diverge by 15–20% of each other and the set is measuring muscular
endurance rather than maximal strength. The set is still stored and still shown
in your history; it simply produces no estimate. Reporting a number we would
immediately have to disclaim is worse than reporting none.

**Limitation.** Both formulas are accurate to roughly ±5% between 2 and 10
reps *for a population*. For an individual, rep-max ratios vary with muscle
fibre composition and with the lift — most people grind out more reps at a
given percentage on a squat than on a deadlift. Treat an estimated 1RM as an
estimate, not as a lift you have made.

---

## Step 3 — Bodyweight normalisation (DOTS)

```
P(bw)  = a + b·bw + c·bw² + d·bw³ + e·bw⁴
DOTS   = estimated 1RM × 500 / P(bw)
```

| Coefficient | Men | Women |
|---|---|---|
| a | −307.75076 | −57.96288 |
| b | 24.0900756 | 13.6175032 |
| c | −0.1918759221 | −0.1126655495 |
| d | 0.0007391293 | 0.0005158568 |
| e | −0.000001093 | −0.0000010706 |

DOTS is the coefficient most powerlifting federations adopted around 2019–2020
in place of Wilks, which flattered very heavy men and penalised both light and
heavy women. It is a fourth-degree polynomial fitted to competition results, so
the strength-to-mass relationship it encodes is **non-linear** — quite unlike a
plain "times bodyweight" ratio.

Control values, asserted in the test suite:

| | Value |
|---|---|
| `P_men(90)` | 773.27460 |
| `P_women(65)` | 473.71918 |
| `P_women(50)` | 399.03926 |

**Bounds.** The polynomial is fitted on adult competitive bodyweights, roughly
40–210 kg for men and 40–150 kg for women. Outside that range GymRank clamps to
the nearest bound and flags it rather than extrapolating: a quartic fitted on
one range does arbitrary things outside it.

### The limitation that matters most

**DOTS was fitted on the squat + bench + deadlift total, not on isolated
lifts.** GymRank uses it as a per-movement bodyweight normaliser, which the
formula was never validated for.

This is defensible here only because of how the number is used. A DOTS score is
only ever compared against thresholds **for the same exercise and the same
sex**. Within one exercise the coefficient is a monotonic function of bodyweight
and nothing else, so it ranks lifters of different sizes on that exercise
consistently. What you may **not** do with these numbers is compare a bench
DOTS to a squat DOTS and conclude anything: the app never does, and the
interface never puts two exercises' DOTS scores side by side as if they were
the same quantity.

---

## Step 4 — Age adjustment

```
age-adjusted score = DOTS × age coefficient
```

Age is taken **as of the day of the performance**, not today, so last year's
training is scored against last year's you.

| Age | Coefficient | Table |
|---|---|---|
| 14–23 | 1.23 down to 1.00 | Foster |
| 24–39 | 1.00 | peak maximal strength; no adjustment |
| 40–90 | 1.000 up to 2.549 | McCulloch |

Both are the tables used by USA Powerlifting. Below 14 no published table
applies, so account creation is refused: pre-pubescent minors fall outside every
one of these curves, and they are not an audience to push toward one-rep-max
work. Above 90 the value for 90 is used.

**Limitation.** These coefficients describe how a *population* of competitive
lifters declines with age. Individual decline varies enormously with training
history, health and hormones. A 60-year-old's ×1.340 is a statement about
60-year-olds in general, not a measurement of what age has cost you.

---

## Step 5 — Strength index, 0 to 1000

Raw DOTS points are not an intuitive scale, and their range differs per
exercise. The index maps them onto a fixed 0–1000 scale by interpolating
between six calibrated anchors.

**Calibration.** For each exercise and sex, six anchor levels are expressed as
bodyweight multiples at a reference bodyweight — 90 kg for men, 65 kg for women.
`calibrate.js` converts each multiple to kilograms at that reference weight and
then to DOTS points using that sex's polynomial. Those DOTS values, not the raw
multiples, are what the ranking interpolates between. The result is that the
non-linearity of the strength-to-mass relationship is carried by the DOTS curve
rather than by a straight proportion.

Men's back squat, for example:

| Level | × bodyweight | kg at 90 kg | DOTS | Index |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Untrained | 0.75 | 67.5 | 43.6 | 100 |
| Novice | 1.25 | 112.5 | 72.7 | 250 |
| Intermediate | 1.75 | 157.5 | 101.8 | 450 |
| Advanced | 2.50 | 225.0 | 145.5 | 650 |
| Elite | 3.00 | 270.0 | 174.6 | 825 |
| World-class | 3.60 | 324.0 | 209.5 | 1000 |

Between anchors the index is linear; below Untrained it interpolates down to
zero; above World-class it caps at 1000.

The uneven index spacing is deliberate. The first 250 points arrive within
weeks of starting, so the app is encouraging early. Each subsequent tier costs
progressively more real kilograms, and the Elite → World-class gap is wide
enough that 1000 is unreachable without competing internationally. A 90 kg man
needs about **318 kg** on the squat to enter the top rank, and 67.5 kg to leave
Iron.

**Limitation — and this is the softest part of the whole system.** The DOTS
polynomial and the age tables are published, versioned artefacts used by
federations. The anchor multiples are not: they come from the strength-standard
tables that circulate widely in lifting, which are compiled from gym data of
unknown provenance and vary between sources. They are reasonable and widely
recognised, but they are convention, not measurement. If your rank feels a
division too generous or too harsh, this is almost certainly why — and it is a
one-line change in `scoring-config.js`.

---

## Step 6 — Ranks and divisions

| Rank | Index | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Iron | 0–99 | untrained |
| Bronze | 100–249 | beginner |
| Silver | 250–449 | around six months of consistent training |
| Gold | 450–649 | intermediate, one to two years |
| Platinum | 650–824 | advanced, three to five years |
| Diamond | 825–924 | amateur elite |
| Master | 925–979 | national-level competitor |
| Unkillable Demon King | 980–1000 | world-class |

Each rank splits into five equal divisions, **V** to **I**, with I the highest —
about forty steps in all. Enough small wins to stay motivated, with the summit
kept far away.

The division arithmetic is `(max + 1 − min) / 5`, so Silver's 200 points give
divisions of 40: Silver V is 250–289, Silver I is 410–449. The displayed index
is always rounded **down**, because rounding 409.5 up to 410 beside a Silver II
badge — when Silver I begins at 410 — reads as a bug.

---

## Step 7 — The overall rank

```
for each exercise whose global_weight > 0:
    use the best index of the last 120 days
    if there is none, use the all-time best, decayed
overall = mean weighted by global_weight
```

| Exercise | Weight |
|---|---|
| Squat, bench press, deadlift | 1.0 |
| Overhead press, pull-up | 0.5 |
| Dip, push-up | 0 (tracked, not counted) |

The overall rank appears only once **at least three of the five weighted
exercises** have data. Below that it says so and lists what is missing, rather
than implying a whole-body verdict from one lift.

**Decay.** Beyond the 120-day window a best decays 0.5% per week, compounded,
with a floor at 60% of its value:

| Time since the best | Factor |
|---|---|
| Up to 120 days | 1.000 |
| 127 days | 0.995 |
| 1 year | 0.839 |
| 2 years | 0.646 |
| ~10 years and beyond | 0.600 (floor) |

A *recent* best always wins over a better stale one. If you squatted 220 kg in
January and 170 kg last week, the rank reflects last week. That is the point: in
an app with no leaderboard, decay is the only thing standing between a rank and
a trophy you won once and keep forever. The floor exists so that a long injury
lay-off costs you ranks but never erases your history.

## Guard rails

**Ranges are enforced on the server**, in middleware, not only in the browser:
bodyweight 30–250 kg, height 100–250 cm, load 0–500 kg (negative allowed only as
band assistance on bodyweight movements), reps 1–100, and no performance dated
in the future or before your 14th birthday.

**Implausible jumps are held back.** A set implying a 1RM more than 25% above
your best confirmed effort on that exercise is stored, shown, and **excluded
from your rank** until either a second set reaches 90% of it or you confirm it
by hand. A typo of 300 kg instead of 30 kg would otherwise wreck the progress
curve permanently. Correcting the typo clears the flag automatically.

---

## Two consequences worth understanding

### Your rank changes when your bodyweight changes

Scores are relative to bodyweight, so a new weigh-in moves your ranks. This is
correct and expected. Going from 63.5 kg to 61 kg on a real account moved:

| Exercise | Before | After | |
|---|---|---|---|
| Back squat | 522 | 536 | ↑ |
| Bench press | 524 | 540 | ↑ |
| Deadlift | 483 | 496 | ↑ |
| Overhead press | 486 | 499 | ↑ |
| **Pull-up** | **410** | **390** | **↓** |
| Overall | 494 | 504 | ↑ |

The barbell lifts rise because the same bar is a better lift for a lighter
person. **The pull-up falls**, because a lighter person lifting their own body
is lifting less. Both directions come from the same formula being honest, and
neither is a bug.

It does mean the overall rank can be nudged by cutting weight. In a competitive
app that would be a problem to defend against. Here there is no leaderboard, so
the only person you could mislead is yourself.

### A heavier lifter scores more on bodyweight movements

Because `P(bw)` flattens out as bodyweight rises, `bw / P(bw)` increases. One
strict pull-up is worth:

| Bodyweight | DOTS |
|---|---|
| 60 kg | 50.6 |
| 80 kg | 55.2 |
| 100 kg | 61.6 |
| 130 kg | 72.8 |

The curve is recognising that hauling 130 kg over a bar is harder than hauling
60 kg, even measured against the lifter's own mass. The same mechanism produces
reference case 3 below.

## The three reference cases

These are the worked examples the test suite asserts.

### Case 1 — Man, 25, 90 kg. Bench press 100 kg × 5

| | |
|---|---|
| Effective load | 100 kg |
| Epley | 100 × (1 + 5/30) = 116.67 kg |
| Brzycki | 100 × 36/32 = 112.50 kg |
| Estimated 1RM | 114.58 kg |
| DOTS | 114.58 × 500 / 773.27 = **74.09** |
| Age coefficient | ×1.00 (age 24–39) |
| Index | between Intermediate (72.7) and Advanced (101.8) → **459.3** |
| Rank | **Gold V** |

### Case 2 — Woman, 20, 50 kg. One strict pull-up

| | |
|---|---|
| Effective load | 1.00 × 50 + 0 = 50 kg |
| Estimated 1RM | 50 kg (a single rep is its own maximum) |
| DOTS | 50 × 500 / 399.04 = **62.65** |
| Age coefficient | ×1.03 (Foster, age 20) |
| Age-adjusted | 64.53 |
| Index | **190.6** |
| Rank | **Bronze II** |

### Case 3 — Man, 60, 90 kg. One strict pull-up

| | |
|---|---|
| Effective load | 1.00 × 90 + 0 = 90 kg |
| Estimated 1RM | 90 kg |
| DOTS | 90 × 500 / 773.27 = **58.19** |
| Age coefficient | ×1.340 (McCulloch, age 60) |
| Age-adjusted | 77.98 |
| Index | **402.0** |
| Rank | **Silver II** |

Cases 2 and 3 are the whole argument for the system. The same apparent feat —
one strict pull-up — is worth 190 for the young light woman and 402 for the
older heavier man, because he is moving 80% more mass at an age where strength
has measurably declined. A logbook that recorded "1 pull-up" for both would be
telling you nothing.

### Two corrections to our own specification

Our written specification predicted case 1 at index ~430 and Gold IV, and case 3
at Silver I. Both were wrong, and the engine disagreeing with them is how we
found out.

- **Case 1** produces **459.3**, not 430. The estimated 1RM and DOTS score match
  the specification exactly, so the divergence is in the interpolation step
  alone: 74.09 sits 4.6% of the way from Intermediate (72.7 DOTS, index 450) to
  Advanced (101.8, index 650), giving 450 + 9.3. Separately, 430 would not have
  been Gold IV under any reading — Gold begins at 450, so 430 is Silver I.
- **Case 3** produces **402.0**, which matches the specification's "~400". Only
  the label was wrong: Silver I begins at 410, so 402 is Silver II.

We corrected the expected values rather than the constants. The constants
reproduce all three published DOTS control values to five decimal places, so
they were not the thing in doubt.

## What this is not

GymRank estimates. It does not measure. Every number here rests on
population-level statistics applied to one person, and the weakest link — the
strength-standard anchors — is the one that decides which badge you see.

It is not medical advice, not a training programme, and not a reason to attempt
a one-rep max. If the number disagrees with how strong you feel, the number is
the thing that is more likely to be wrong.
